# Framework notes: EU AI Act Article 4 and the AILit framework

Access date for all sources: **2026-10-25**. This document records what each framework does and does not require, so the curriculum can make honest claims. Written by the build agent with AI assistance; facts checked against the primary sources listed in [sources.md](sources.md).

## 1. EU AI Act, Article 4 (AI literacy) — as amended

### Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2 Feb 2025 | Article 4 entered into application (with prohibitions and AI literacy). |
| Mid-July 2026 | Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force; Article 4 amended (facilitated compliance). |
| 27 Jul 2026 | Consolidated text shown on the AI Act Service Desk (accessed 2026-10-25). |
| 2 Aug 2026 | Supervision and enforcement by national market surveillance authorities began. |

### The amended obligation (structure)

- **Article 4(1)** — Providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy of their staff and of other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf, taking into account their technical knowledge, experience, education and training, and the context the systems are to be used in, and the persons or groups of persons on whom the systems are to be used.
  - **Amended addition:** "This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual."
- **Article 4(2)** — The Commission and the Member States shall support and facilitate the efforts of providers and deployers (especially SMEs); the Commission publishes practical compliance examples.
- **Article 4(3)** — The Board shall adopt recommendations, taking into account European competence frameworks, including by setting out common objectives.

### What Article 4 does NOT require (per the EC Q&A and Service Desk)

- **No specific or "sufficient" level** of AI literacy is mandated, and no measurement of employees' knowledge is required.
- **No mandatory format**: no one-size-fits-all training; formal training is not required by itself (though relying only on user instructions is often ineffective).
- **No certificates**, no required governance structure, no "AI officer".
- **No sector-specific requirements**, but the sector context and the risk level of the systems used matter; high-risk deployers keep separate duties under Article 26 (human oversight training).
- Replicating practices from the Commission's "living repository" of 40+ AI literacy practices does **not** create a presumption of compliance.

### Design implications for this program

1. The stakeholder request "we need AI training for everyone by Q1 so we're covered" is reframed: Article 4 is the **trigger**, but the performance goal is staff using AI outputs safely and correctly in real tasks. A certificate does not demonstrate literacy.
2. Measures must be **proportionate and tailored** (role, context, affected persons) — hence different content depth for customer service vs. HR in the same program, and a job aid for the moment of need.
3. Wording discipline: the program is "**designed to support** Article 4 AI literacy measures". Never "compliant", "certified", or "covers your Art. 4 obligations".

## 2. OECD/EC AILit framework — final version

**Publication:** *Empowering Learners for the Age of AI: An AI Literacy Framework for Primary and Secondary Education*, joint European Commission / OECD initiative, final version published June 2026 (draft published May 2025; feedback from 2,000+ people shaped the final). It also feeds the PISA 2029 Innovative Domain assessment.

### The four domains (final names) and 19 competences

The draft framework named domains "Engaging with AI / Creating with AI / Managing AI / Designing AI". **The final version renamed the fourth domain to "Shape AI"** — an important correction for anyone mapping to the framework.

**Engage with AI** (7 competences)
1. Recognise AI's role and influence in different contexts.
2. Describe how AI systems perform tasks using language that addresses and clarifies common misconceptions.
3. Evaluate whether AI outputs should be accepted, revised or rejected.
4. Examine how predictive AI systems provide recommendations that can inform or limit perspectives.
5. Compare how AI systems consume energy and natural resources.
6. Explain how AI could be used to amplify societal biases.
7. Analyse how well the use of an AI system aligns with ethical principles and human values.

**Create with AI** (4 competences)
1. Use AI systems to explore new perspectives and approaches that build upon original ideas.
2. Visualise, prototype and combine ideas using different types of AI systems.
3. Direct generative AI systems to elicit feedback, refine results and support reflection.
4. Analyse how AI can safeguard or violate content authenticity and intellectual property.

**Manage AI** (4 competences)
1. Decide whether to use AI systems based on the nature of the task.
2. Choose an appropriate AI approach for a task by comparing how different AI systems operate and what they are best suited to do.
3. Decompose a problem to determine when and how AI systems should be used to automate or augment tasks.
4. Monitor and evaluate AI use throughout a problem-solving process.

**Shape AI** (4 competences)
1. Investigate how an AI system is intended to work, whom it is designed for and what its limitations are.
2. Evaluate AI systems using defined criteria, expected outcomes, test cases and user feedback.
3. Design AI systems with attention to how data sources, selection and information flow influence behaviour and outputs.
4. Improve AI systems to address and promote human well-being and societal benefit.

### Progression model

Each competence has three progression levels — **Basic, Intermediate, Advanced** — each with learning scenarios and knowledge references. The domains are presented sequentially to reflect a potential learning approach.

### Scope note and honest framing

AILit is a framework **for primary and secondary education**. This program uses it directly for the school track and treats it as a shared reference for the workplace track (the domains transfer; the scenarios do not). The mapping in [framework-mapping.md](../docs/design/framework-mapping.md) states coverage and gaps honestly — e.g., this program does not address energy consumption of AI (Engage 5) or building/improving AI systems (Shape 3–4), which are out of scope for a judgment-focused program.

## 3. Where the two frameworks meet

- Article 4(3) explicitly directs the AI Board to consider **European competence frameworks** when setting out common objectives — AILit is the obvious candidate for school-age and increasingly for general literacy.
- Both frameworks define AI literacy as more than tool skill: knowledge + skills + attitudes (AILit), proportionate measures fitted to context and affected persons (Article 4).
- The differentiator of this program (verification, data judgment, bias awareness, oversight) maps to **Engage 3, Manage 1–4, and Engage 6** in AILit terms, and to the "context of use / persons affected" tailoring in Article 4.
