# Secondary research: workplace AI use and what training does (and doesn't) address

**Method note.** The original project plan allowed for 3–5 short interviews. This portfolio replaces first-hand interviews with **secondary research**: a synthesis of published surveys and reports on workplace AI use, with full citations. This is real, citable evidence about the population — but it is not first-hand, and it describes populations, not the fictional client. All sources accessed **2026-10-25**; full annotations in [sources.md](sources.md).

The fictional client, Northwind Logistics, and all personas are **fictional**. The research below grounds the design assumptions; it does not describe Northwind.

---

## 1. Use is already widespread — and mostly self-taught

- **75% of knowledge workers** globally already use generative AI at work, and **46% of users started within the previous six months** (Microsoft & LinkedIn 2024 Work Trend Index; 31,000 respondents across 31 countries). Most of these users bring their own tools rather than waiting for IT.
- **88% of respondent organizations** report using AI in at least one business function (McKinsey, State of AI, November 2025), though scaling beyond pilots remains uneven.

**Design implication:** a "how to use the tool" course arrives too late. The audience is already using AI daily and untrained. The workplace track therefore starts where people are — Module 1 asks "where is AI already in my work?" — and invests its 80 minutes in judgment (verification, data, oversight), not in tool features.

## 2. Demand is vague: capability wanted, specificity missing

- AI appears in **43.3% of L&D job postings** in the most recent period (up from 14.6% before 2026), but only **8.3%** of those postings name a specific generative AI tool, model or practice (Teamed, *L&D Skills Landscape 2026*).

**Design implication:** organizations want "AI capability" but cannot yet specify what it means. This program's answer is a **concrete behavioral specification**: the needs analysis defines five critical behaviors (see [needs analysis](../docs/design/needs-analysis.md)) and every module objective is observable and assessed. This is what "AI capability" should mean for a general workforce: make correct verify/paste/escalate decisions.

## 3. Training skews toward productivity; risk and judgment are under-served

- Interface (EU) finds corporate AI training "often prioritises productivity and tool use", while responsible implementation also requires understanding of limitations, failure modes, potential harms and applicable obligations. It further finds that **validated assessment methods** for workplace AI literacy are lacking — few validated scales exist, and none tested across cultures (Lintner 2024).
- Note the precise claim: the gap is in **measurement and emphasis**, not a claim that no program works.

**Design implication:** the program differentiates exactly here — verification routines, data classification, bias awareness and escalation are the spine of all four modules, and assessment is scenario-based (decision-making) rather than definition recall, because validated instruments don't exist yet and self-report is weak evidence.

## 4. Policy context: obligation without a bar

- Article 4 of the EU AI Act has applied since 2 February 2025 and was amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI (in force mid-July 2026; enforcement by national market surveillance authorities from 2 August 2026). The amended Article 4(1) obliges providers and deployers to take measures to support AI literacy, tailored to knowledge, training, context of use and affected persons — and explicitly does **not** require any individual to reach a specific level (AI Act Service Desk; EC policy page; EC Q&A — see [framework notes](framework-notes.md)).

**Design implication:** "we're covered" is the wrong target; "our staff make safe decisions" is the right one. The evaluation plan (LTEM Tier 5) targets decision-making, not certificates.

## 5. What secondary research cannot tell us

- These surveys describe broad populations, not Northwind's actual roles, tools or data rules. The fictional needs analysis therefore marks population-level facts as external evidence and flags the client-specific claims (e.g., "staff paste customer data into AI") as **hypothetical for a fictional client**.
- No first-hand data was collected for this portfolio. A real deployment would add interviews and the pilot protocol in `evaluation/human-protocol/` (consent text, pre/post forms, think-aloud script) — kept ready in the repository.
