# Teacher guide

For teachers of students aged 14–16 (Grade 9–10 / Year 10–11). **You do not need any AI background to teach this unit.** Every technical answer you might be asked for is in the misconception table below.

*Last reviewed: 2026-10-25*

Each lesson: **50 minutes**, structured as Hook (5) → Main activity (25) → Discussion (10) → Exit ticket (5) → Buffer (5).

## Prerequisites and requirements

- **Prerequisites: none.** No prior AI knowledge for students or teacher.
- **Tech:** one device with a browser per pair for Lessons 1–2 (the [interactive](../interactives/next-word/index.html) runs offline, no accounts, no network). Lesson 3 works best if you can show two short AI outputs you prepared; Lesson 4 needs nothing.
- **No devices?** Every lesson has an unplugged fallback (marked "No devices" in each lesson plan). The core of Lesson 1 works on paper: students play the predictor themselves.
- **Safety:** the unit asks students to **discuss** AI, not to use it for assignments. Nothing in this unit requires students to have accounts with any AI service.

## How to talk about students' personal AI use — without judgment

Many students use chatbots for homework. The unit works only if students can say so honestly. Three rules for yourself as the teacher:

1. **Never ask "who here uses AI to cheat?"** Ask "when does AI help you learn?" — the same behavior gets discussed without confessions.
2. **The target is thinking, not tools.** The unit's message: *using AI is a decision about your learning*, not a rule handed down. Lesson 4 lets students write their own rules — keep that frame even if a rule is laxer than you would like; the reflection is the point.
3. **No anecdotes about getting caught.** Fear shuts the discussion down; you need it open for Lessons 2–4 to land.

## Misconceptions to watch for (and what to say)

| A student says… | The misconception | You say… |
|---|---|---|
| "The chatbot knows things." | Knowledge vs. prediction | "It predicts likely next words from patterns in text it was trained on. It has no notes, no memory of facts — Lesson 1 shows you the machinery." |
| "It cited a website, so it's true." | Citations are proof | "The citation text itself is predicted. Open the link. Does it exist? Does it say that? Lesson 2 is exactly this skill." |
| "It's just a computer, computers can't be unfair." | Neutrality of technology | "It learned from human text, and human text contains unfair patterns. If the training data leans one way, the output leans that way. Lesson 3." |
| "AI is always getting better, so soon it's fine to trust." | Trust as a dial that only goes up | "Capability and trustworthiness are different axes. Fluent wrong answers are the normal failure mode — the check stays yours." |
| "If it sounds sure, it is sure." | Confidence = truth | "The model has no 'sure' — it has 'likely'. You supply the certainty, by checking." |

If a student asks something you cannot answer: **"Good question — I don't know. Let's find out or add it to the parking lot."** Modeling not-knowing is itself AI literacy.

## Differentiation

**Support:**
- Sentence starters on the [fact-check worksheet](handouts/fact-check-worksheet.html) ("This claim says ___. The source says ___.")
- Pair students deliberately: fluent AI users with careful readers, swap roles each lesson.
- Reduce scope: two claims instead of three in Lesson 2; two rules instead of three in Lesson 4.

**Stretch:**
- Lesson 2: find a claim where the source *partially* disagrees — partial truth is the interesting case.
- Lesson 3: propose one concrete change to how a model's training data could be picked to be fairer, and its trade-off.
- Lesson 4: write a rule for AI use *during exams vs. homework* that is defensible to both teacher and students.
- Early finishers: run the [interactive](../interactives/next-word/index.html) at extreme temperature and explain what changes in one paragraph.

## Answer keys

- **Lesson 1** (recording sheet): "surprise" words at high temperature are *expected*, not errors — the model picks unlikely words on purpose sometimes (that is what the slider does). A chatbot "knowing" things is false; it predicts.
- **Lesson 2** (worked example on the worksheet): the sample chatbot answer contains (1) an invented statistic "in 1842 the bridge collapsed twice", (2) a plausible but non-existent book source, (3) a correct-sounding but unverified date. Students need **two** of three to meet the objective.
- **Lesson 3** (comparison pairs): there is no single right answer — assess whether the stated *cause* (training data, who wrote the sources, default perspectives) matches the *example*.
- **Lesson 4** (scenario sort): scenarios 1 and 4 → AI helps learning; scenario 2 (essay ghostwriting) → replaces thinking; scenario 3 (exam practice answers before trying) → replaces thinking **unless** the rule says "try first, then check". Accept any rule with a reason.

## Assessment in this unit

- Exit tickets (Lessons 1–4) check the four objectives informally.
- The [fact-check worksheet](handouts/fact-check-worksheet.html) is the Lesson 2 artifact.
- [Final project](final-project.html) covers all four objectives, scored with the shared [rubric](../assessment/rubric.html) (criteria adapted for school use as noted there).
- The [performance task](../assessment/performance-task.html) has a school variant using a flawed "AI-written" essay, constructed for teaching purposes.

## One-minute prep checklist (per lesson)

1. Print handouts (or share the printable pages — they print cleanly).
2. Open the [interactive](../interactives/next-word/index.html) in a browser tab (Lesson 1) — it works offline.
3. Re-read the misconception table above.
4. Decide pairs. That's it.
