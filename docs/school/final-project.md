# Final project: The chatbot survival guide

Applies all four objectives (S1.1–S4.1). Students create a guide for a younger student (or a grandparent — their choice of "someone who trusts chatbots too much") explaining how to use a chatbot wisely. Scored with the [rubric](../assessment/rubric.html) (school adaptation noted there) and planned with the [project planner](handouts/project-planner.html).

*Last reviewed: 2026-10-25*

## The brief (student-facing)

> A younger student says: "I just ask the chatbot — it knows everything." You have four lessons of evidence that this is not true. Make them a **survival guide**.
>
> **Format:** a poster, a one-page leaflet, a short video script, or a web page. Paper formats are fine; no account with any AI service is needed.
>
> **It must include:**
> 1. **How it works** — in your own words: what the chatbot actually does (Lesson 1). Include one example of a sentence where the next word is predictable, and one where it isn't.
> 2. **When it lies beautifully** — the claim types that must be checked (numbers, dates, names, sources, superlatives) and **one real fact-check you did yourself**, with your source (Lesson 2).
> 3. **Who it might leave out** — one way outputs can be unfair to a group and what causes it (Lesson 3).
> 4. **Your rules** — two personal rules for using AI for schoolwork, with reasons (Lesson 4).
>
> **Constraints:** 200–300 words (or 90 seconds spoken). Honest tone — no scare tactics; the guide should make the reader *smarter*, not *scared*.
>
> **Cite your AI help:** end with one line saying whether — and how — you used AI while making the guide.

## How the project is scored

Mapped to the [rubric](../assessment/rubric.html):

| Rubric criterion | In this project |
|---|---|
| Accuracy checking | Part 2 — the self-done fact-check with a real source is the core evidence |
| Data judgment | Adapted: *source judgment* — the guide cites where its own facts came from |
| Bias recognition | Part 3 — example and cause must match |
| Justification | Parts 1 and 4 — explanations in the student's own words, reasons for rules |

**What the score does not measure:** design beauty. A plain, correct guide outscores a gorgeous, vague one. Say this out loud when you assign it.

## Suggested timing

| Session | Work |
|---|---|
| Lesson 4, end | Brief + planner started (choice of format, partner choice) |
| Homework or one work period | Draft all four parts; do the fact-check |
| Final 20 minutes (next lesson) | Peer review in pairs against the four rubric criteria, then revise |

## Peer review protocol (the final 20 minutes)

Pairs swap guides and answer three questions on a sticky note:
1. "Which part convinced you most — why?" (names a specific part)
2. "Where did you want a source or example and not find one?"
3. "Is any part scare-tactics instead of explanation? Quote it."

Authors revise using the notes; the teacher scores after revision.

## Common pitfalls to warn about

- **Scare-posters** ("AI WILL RUIN YOUR HOMEWORK") — fails the tone constraint; the brief asks for smarter, not scared.
- **The fact-check that checked nothing** — "I checked it and it was true" without naming the source scores low on criterion 1.
- **Rules without reasons** — "don't use AI for homework, because it's bad" is not a justification; push for the *why*.
- **Copying the lessons back verbatim** — the guide must use the student's own words; that is what "in your own words" is testing.
