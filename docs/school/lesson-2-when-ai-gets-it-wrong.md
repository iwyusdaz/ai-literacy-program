# Lesson 2: When AI gets it wrong

**Core question: Why are wrong answers so convincing?**
Objective: **S2.1** — identify at least two claims that need checking and verify each with a reliable source.
Handout: [Fact-check worksheet](handouts/fact-check-worksheet.html) (includes the worked example and answer key reference)

*Last reviewed: 2026-10-25*

---

## Hook — 5 min

**Teacher script prompt:** read this aloud (do **not** fact-check it yet):

> "The Great Fire of London started in 1666 in a bakery on Pudding Lane and destroyed 13,000 homes. The city was rebuilt in exactly five years, funded by the King's personal fortune of two million pounds."

Ask: "How many of you think all four facts in there are right?" (Show of hands.) "Now — how do you *know* you think that? You checked nothing. It *sounded* right. That feeling has a name: fluency. Today we build the habit that beats it."

## Main activity — 25 min

### The fact-check challenge (pairs)

Each pair gets a chatbot-style answer (below) and the [worksheet](handouts/fact-check-worksheet.html). Rules:

1. **Read it once, normally.** Mark with a pencil: T (sounds true), ? (not sure), F (sounds false).
2. **Now find at least two claims that need checking.** Claims to check = claims with a number, a date, a name, a source, or a superlative ("the first", "the only", "exactly").
3. **Verify each claim with a reliable source** (textbook, library site, official website — agree in class which sources count).
4. Record on the worksheet: *claim → what the source says → verdict* (correct / wrong / can't tell).

### The sample answer to check (also printed on the worksheet)

> "The Great Wall of China is the only man-made structure visible from space with the naked eye. It stretches exactly 21,196 kilometers. The wall was built mainly to keep out rabbits and deer, a fact documented in the 1998 book *Walls of the Middle Kingdom* by Professor Henry Caldwell."

(Answer key for the teacher: the "visible from space" claim is a famous myth — false; the 21,196 km figure is roughly right for all sections of the wall across dynasties — needs the source named; the rabbit/deer claim is invented; the 1998 book does not exist. Students need **two verified claims** to meet the objective; the conversation about *why* the false ones sounded fine is the real lesson.)

### Teacher moves during the activity

- Circulate and ask: "What *kind* of claim is that?" (number/date/name/source/superlative → check it)
- When a pair finds a false claim, ask: "Did your first pencil mark say T or F?" Most will have said T. That is the hook for the discussion.

## Discussion — 10 min

- "Why are wrong answers so convincing?" → Because they are built from *likely* text — they sound exactly like true text. Tone carries no truth signal.
- "What types of claims must always be checked?" → Numbers, dates, names, sources, superlatives. Collect the class list on the board — this list is Lesson 4's rule material.
- "What do you do when the source *partially* disagrees?" → Stretch pairs share; note that "mostly right with one wrong number" is the most dangerous case of all.

## Exit ticket — 5 min

One sentence: "A chatbot answer sounds convincing. Name the two things you will check before you use it."

## No devices

No devices needed at any point — verification uses printed sources or the library. If sources are scarce, the teacher pre-prints 4–5 "source cards" (true reference pages) and pairs must match their claims to the right card.

## Differentiation

**Support:** worksheet has sentence frames ("The claim says ___. The source says ___. So the claim is ___.") and asks for two claims, not three.
**Stretch:** find the *partially true* claim and write why partial truth is harder to spot than total nonsense; or bring your own chatbot answer from home and fact-check it (personal AI use discussed without judgment — see the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.html)).
