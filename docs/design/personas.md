# Personas

**All personas are fictional.** They are design tools synthesized from the [secondary research](https://github.com/iwyusdaz/ai-literacy-program/blob/main/research/secondary-research.md) and the needs analysis — not real people, not survey respondents. Quotes are invented to summarize each persona's situation.

*Last reviewed: 2026-10-25*

## Workplace track

### Lena, 27 — customer service agent

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Context | Drafts customer replies daily with the new AI assistant at Northwind Logistics |
| Prior AI use | Daily, uncritical — trusts fluent output |
| Key risk | Pastes customer data into prompts; sends unverified claims ("we always deliver within 48 hours") |
| What she needs | A fast verification routine that fits her handle time; clear data rules |

> "The AI writes better than I do, and it's never wrong in an obvious way."

**Design implications:** Module 2 must give a *checklist that takes seconds*, not a philosophy of epistemology. Module 3 must give a bright-line rule she can apply under time pressure. Quiz items for her use customer-reply scenarios with time pressure in the stem.

### Joris, 45 — operations planner

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Context | Uses AI to summarize weekly operations and delivery reports |
| Prior AI use | Occasional, skeptical |
| Key risk | Trusts numbers that appear in AI summaries — they look authoritative but may be invented |
| What he needs | Knowing where AI fails with numbers; a concrete check for figures |

> "I don't trust the chatbot's opinions. Numbers are numbers though."

**Design implication:** Module 2 needs an explicit "AI and numbers" section: invented figures, wrong totals, plausible-looking tables. His practice exercise is a summary with three fabricated numbers.

### Sofia, 38 — HR administrator

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Context | Considering AI to screen incoming CVs |
| Prior AI use | Low |
| Key risk | Bias against groups; decisions about people delegated to a system; unclear legal context |
| What she needs | Recognizing high-risk use; knowing who decides; an escalation path |

> "If it saves the team a day of screening, why not?"

**Design implication:** Module 4 centers on her scenario (the branching mini-scenario "The HR shortcut"). The content must avoid legal jargon but name the principle: the more a decision affects a person, the more a human belongs in the loop.

## School track

### Ayo, 15 — student

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Context | Uses chatbots for homework several times a week |
| Prior AI use | High, fluent |
| Key risk | Offloading thinking; trusting fluent text; not understanding *why* answers are wrong |
| What he needs | To see how the text is produced (prediction), and rules for when AI helps learning |

> "It just knows things. If it's wrong, why does it sound so sure?"

**Design implication:** Lesson 1 must produce a genuine "aha" (he *makes* the model choose next words in the [interactive](../interactives/next-word/index.html)). Lesson 4 must not moralize — it co-designs rules with students.

### Mr. Daniels — teacher (non-specialist)

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Context | Teaches the unit as part of the curriculum; IT background is basic |
| Prior AI use | Low–medium |
| Key risk | Low confidence; fears student questions he cannot answer |
| What he needs | A ready-to-teach [guide](../school/teacher-guide.html) with answers, timings, and "if a student asks X, say Y" support |

> "I can't answer technical questions about AI. I need the answers in front of me."

**Design implication:** Every lesson plan includes a misconception table with suggested responses, exact timings, and an unplugged fallback. Nothing requires the teacher to improvise technical content.
